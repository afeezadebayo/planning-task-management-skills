# Habits and Routines

Most habit plans start big and fade within weeks: a new morning routine with eight steps, a daily workout, an hour of reading, all at once. Research on habit formation shows why patience matters: in a well-known study by Phillippa Lally and colleagues, new daily behaviours took a median of 66 days to become automatic, with a range from 18 to 254 days. [Research, Lally et al. 2010] Missing a single day did not undo progress. Habits form through repetition in a stable context, not through willpower spikes.

This skill designs habits and routines that last: choosing one or two habits that serve a goal, starting small, anchoring them to existing routines, designing the environment, planning for obstacles, tracking lightly and reviewing weekly. It also builds routines such as morning starts, end-of-day shutdowns and weekly resets.

## Operating principles
1. **One or two habits at a time.** Add the next only when the first feels automatic.
2. **Start tiny.** A version so small it is hard to skip, then grow it.
3. **Anchor to a cue.** Attach the habit to something that already happens daily, an approach popularised as habit stacking.
4. **Design the environment.** Make the good habit easier and the unwanted habit harder.
5. **Never miss twice.** Missing once is normal; get back the next day.

## Habit design template
```
Goal it serves: <goal or key result>
Habit: <specific behaviour>
Tiny version: <2-minute version>
Cue (after I...): <existing routine or time and place>
If-then obstacle plan: If <obstacle>, then <alternative>
Environment changes: <make it easy / make the opposite hard>
Tracking: <simple tick or count>
Review: <weekly review question>
```

Example: "After I make my morning coffee, I write for 10 minutes at the kitchen table. If I travel, I write 5 minutes on my phone notes."

## Routines worth building
| Routine | Typical steps | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Morning start (10 to 20 minutes) | Review calendar, pick top three tasks, first focus block | Starts the day on priorities, not messages |
| Daily shutdown (10 minutes) | Capture loose tasks, update list, plan tomorrow's first task | Ends work clearly and reduces evening worry |
| Weekly reset (30 to 45 minutes) | Weekly review, plan priorities, tidy workspace | Keeps the system trusted |
| Focus routine | Same place, phone away, one task, timer | Lowers the cost of starting deep work |
| Evening wind-down | Screens off earlier, prepare tomorrow | Supports sleep, which supports everything else |

## When to use, and when not to
Use it when someone wants to build or break a habit, design a morning or shutdown routine, connect daily behaviour to goals, or restart routines after disruption.

Don't use it for clinical issues such as addiction, eating disorders, sleep disorders or mental health conditions; suggest speaking to a qualified professional. Avoid extreme targets, punishing routines or rigid rules that encourage unhealthy behaviour.

## Inputs
- The goal the habit serves.
- Current daily schedule and existing routines (cues).
- Past attempts and what got in the way.
- Environment: home, office, travel patterns.
- Preferred tracking: app, calendar, paper or agent check-in.

## Workflow

### Step 1. Choose the habit
Pick one or two behaviours that most directly serve a goal. Make each specific: "walk 20 minutes after lunch", not "be healthier".

### Step 2. Shrink it
Design a tiny version for hard days, such as one page, one push-up or five minutes.

### Step 3. Find the cue
An existing routine or a fixed time and place. "After I close my laptop at 6pm, I put on my walking shoes."

### Step 4. Design the environment
Put what is needed in sight (book on the pillow, shoes by the door); add friction to unwanted habits (apps logged out, phone in another room).

### Step 5. Plan for obstacles
List the likely obstacles (travel, late meetings, low energy) and an if-then response for each.

### Step 6. Track lightly
A tick per day in a calendar or the agent's daily check. Track the habit, not perfection.

### Step 7. Review weekly
During the weekly review: how many days, what got in the way, what to adjust. Grow the habit only after two or three steady weeks.

## Breaking an unwanted habit
- Identify the cue, the routine and the reward it gives.
- Remove or avoid the cue where possible (notifications off, snacks out of sight).
- Replace the routine with one that gives a similar reward (a short walk instead of scrolling during breaks).
- Add friction: log out, move apps, use blockers during focus time.
- Expect slips; record the trigger and adjust the plan.

## Agent check-ins
An AI agent can help without nagging: a short daily prompt at the chosen time, a weekly summary of streaks and misses, suggestions to shrink the habit after two misses in a row, and celebration of milestones such as 30 days. Users choose the tone and can switch check-ins off.

## Output format
```
1. Habit plan(s) using the template
2. Routine designs (morning, shutdown, weekly reset) as needed
3. Environment changes
4. Obstacle plans
5. Tracking method and weekly review questions
6. When to grow the habit or add the next one
```

## Daily shutdown script
```
1. Capture any loose tasks from notes, chat and email
2. Tick done items; move unfinished ones to tomorrow or later
3. Check tomorrow's calendar for preparation needed
4. Choose tomorrow's first task
5. Close tabs and apps; say or write "shutdown complete"
```
A clear end to the workday helps people stop thinking about unfinished work in the evening, because the next step is already written down.

## Measuring progress
Count days completed per week rather than unbroken streaks, which break easily and can discourage. Five out of seven is a strong week. After a month, ask whether the habit is moving the goal it serves; if not, change the habit rather than adding effort.

## Restarting after a break
Illness, travel or busy seasons interrupt every routine. Restart with the tiny version for a week, pick the same cue as before, and review what made the habit drop so the plan can absorb it next time.

Keep the same cue where possible, because the context is part of what makes a habit automatic.

## Edge cases and judgement calls
| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| Several habits wanted at once | Pick one; queue the rest |
| Shift work or irregular days | Use event cues ("after my first break") rather than clock times |
| Travel-heavy weeks | Keep the tiny version only |
| All-or-nothing thinking | Count partial days as wins |
| Habit linked to health conditions | Encourage advice from a qualified professional |

## Worked example
A freelance designer wants to build a portfolio but never finds time. The habit plan: after the morning coffee, 20 minutes of portfolio work at the desk, with a tiny version of five minutes on busy days. The laptop opens to the portfolio file the night before (environment), and a travel plan allows sketching on paper. The agent checks in at 8:30 each day and summarises on Fridays. After three weeks at five or more days a week, the session grows to 40 minutes, and the portfolio is finished in two months.

## Common mistakes
- Starting several habits at once.
- Big habits with no tiny version.
- No cue, so the habit depends on memory.
- Quitting after one missed day.
- Tracking so detailed it becomes a chore.

## Self-check before you answer
- Is there one specific habit with a tiny version?
- Is it anchored to a reliable cue?
- Are obstacles and environment covered?
- Is tracking light and reviewed weekly?
