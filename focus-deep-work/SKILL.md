# Focus and Deep Work

Knowledge work rewards long stretches of concentration, yet most workdays are cut into fragments by meetings, messages and notifications. Cal Newport's book Deep Work argues that the ability to focus without distraction on hard tasks is increasingly rare and valuable. [Reference, Deep Work] Research by Gloria Mark and colleagues found people interrupted during tasks compensated by working faster, but reported more stress and frustration. [Research, Mark et al. 2008] Sophie Leroy described "attention residue": after switching tasks, part of the mind stays on the previous one, lowering performance on the next. [Research, Leroy 2009]

This skill helps people protect and use focused time: choosing the work that deserves it, scheduling focus blocks, reducing interruptions, preparing a focus routine, handling messages without losing the thread, and reviewing what worked. It balances focus with real job demands such as support, management and collaboration.

## Operating principles
1. **Focus is scheduled, not found.** Book it like a meeting before the week fills.
2. **One task per block.** Decide the task before the block starts.
3. **Remove triggers, not just resist them.** Notifications off, phone away, tabs closed.
4. **Batch the shallow work.** Email and chat in set windows, not all day.
5. **Fit the role.** A support lead and a writer need different focus patterns.

## Focus patterns
| Pattern | How it works | Fits |
|---|---|---|
| Daily focus block | 90 to 120 minutes at the same time each day | Most knowledge workers |
| Focus days | One or two meeting-free days a week | Makers, writers, engineers |
| Sprints | 25 to 50 minutes of focus, 5 to 10 minutes break, repeated | Interruptible roles, getting started |
| Seasonal | Weeks of intense focus between busy periods | Researchers, founders, writers |
| Team focus hours | Whole team pauses meetings and chat for set hours | Teams sharing calendars |

## When to use, and when not to
Use it when important work keeps getting pushed aside, the day is fragmented, notifications dominate, a big piece of thinking work is due, or a team wants protected focus time.

Don't use it to treat attention difficulties that may need assessment; suggest a qualified professional while still offering practical strategies. Don't use it to ignore real responsibilities such as on-call duties or customer commitments.

## Inputs
- The important work that needs focus.
- Calendar and typical meeting pattern.
- Main sources of interruption (chat, email, phone, colleagues, home).
- Energy pattern: when the person thinks best.
- Role demands: response expectations, on-call, management.

## Workflow

### Step 1. Choose deep-work tasks
List work that needs sustained thinking: writing, analysis, strategy, design, coding, learning. Link each to a goal.

### Step 2. Pick the pattern and times
Match the pattern to the role; place blocks in high-energy hours.

### Step 3. Book and protect the blocks
Recurring calendar events with a clear title, such as "Focus: Q4 report". Decline or move meetings that collide, with alternatives offered.

### Step 4. Set expectations
Tell the team when focus blocks happen and how to reach the person for real emergencies. A status message such as "Focusing until 11, call for urgent issues" works well.

### Step 5. Prepare the environment
Notifications off, phone in another room, one window open, materials ready, water nearby, a notepad to park stray thoughts.

### Step 6. Start with a ritual
The same short start each time: open the task, write the goal for the block, set a timer.

### Step 7. Handle interruptions
Park thoughts on the notepad; check messages only at the end of the block; for unavoidable interruptions, write one line about where you were before switching to reduce attention residue.

### Step 8. Close and review
At the end, note what was done and the next step. Weekly, count focus hours and adjust.

## Interruption reduction checklist
- Turn off non-essential notifications on desktop and phone.
- Close email and chat during focus blocks.
- Use do-not-disturb and focus modes, with exceptions for key people.
- Batch email and chat into two to four windows a day.
- Agree response-time norms with the team (for example, chat replies within two hours, urgent issues by phone).
- Move recurring meetings to one part of the day.
- Use website or app blockers if needed.

## Output format
```
1. Deep-work tasks linked to goals
2. Focus pattern and weekly schedule
3. Calendar blocks and meeting changes
4. Team message on focus times and urgent contact
5. Environment and start ritual
6. Message batching windows and response norms
7. Weekly focus hours target and review question
```

## Using AI agents during focus time
Agents can protect focus as well as break it. Useful patterns: ask the agent to prepare materials and summaries before the block starts, hand it research questions to answer while you work on something else, and review its output after the block rather than checking in constantly. Turn off agent notifications during focus time, just like chat.

## Breaks and energy
Focus depends on energy. Short breaks between blocks, a walk, water and daylight help people return sharper. Two to four hours of real deep work a day is a strong result for most people; trying to force eight usually backfires. When concentration fades early every day, check sleep, workload and stress before adding more techniques.

## Team focus hours
Teams can agree shared focus hours, for example 9 to 11 on Tuesdays and Thursdays, with no meetings and chat on pause except for incidents. Write the norm down, add it to calendars, review it monthly and adjust for time zones in distributed teams.

## Working from home
At home, agree focus hours with the people you live with, use a consistent spot that signals work, and keep household tasks out of focus blocks by listing them for a set time later in the day.

## Edge cases and judgement calls
| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| Manager or support role | Shorter blocks, early morning or late afternoon; protect two longer blocks weekly |
| Open-plan office or busy home | Headphones, booked rooms, or focus at quieter times |
| Team culture of instant replies | Agree norms openly; start with one protected block a day |
| Focus blocks keep getting booked over | Mark them busy; move them, never delete them |
| Hard to start | Begin with a 25-minute sprint on the smallest part |
| Energy crashes | Shorter blocks, breaks, movement; check sleep and workload |

## Worked example
An analyst had back-to-back meetings and never finished a quarterly model. The plan moved recurring meetings to afternoons, booked 9 to 11 focus blocks on four days, set chat to do-not-disturb with a status message, batched email at 11:30, 2 and 4:30, and kept a notepad for stray thoughts. The team agreed urgent issues come by phone. Focus hours rose from about three to ten a week, and the model was finished two weeks early.

## Common mistakes
- Waiting for free time instead of booking it.
- Starting a block without choosing the task.
- Leaving notifications on and relying on willpower.
- Checking messages "quickly" mid-block.
- Copying someone else's focus pattern that does not fit the role.

## Measuring focus
Count focus hours each week and the important outputs finished. Aim to grow focus hours steadily rather than jump to an ideal. If hours drop, look for the cause in the weekly review: meetings, interruptions, unclear tasks or fatigue.

## Self-check before you answer
- Are focus blocks booked in high-energy hours?
- Does each block have a chosen task?
- Are interruptions reduced at the source?
- Do the team know the norms and urgent route?
