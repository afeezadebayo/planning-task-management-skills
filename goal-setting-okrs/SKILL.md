# Goal Setting and OKRs

Most goals fail quietly. They are too vague to act on ("grow the business", "get fit"), too many to focus on, disconnected from the weekly plan, or never checked again after January. Objectives and Key Results (OKRs), used at Intel under Andy Grove and popularised by John Doerr's book Measure What Matters, fix part of this by pairing an inspiring objective with a few measurable results. [Reference, Measure What Matters] Research on implementation intentions adds the other part: goals linked to specific if-then plans are more likely to be reached. [Research, Gollwitzer and Sheeran 2006]

This skill sets personal, career or small-team goals that work: a few clear objectives, measurable key results, the projects and habits that drive them, if-then plans, a check-in rhythm and fair scoring at the end of the period.

## Operating principles
1. **Few goals.** One to three objectives per quarter for a person; three to five for a team.
2. **Outcomes, not activities.** Key results measure change ("activation rate from 30% to 40%"), not effort ("send 10 emails").
3. **Connect to the week.** Every key result has projects or habits that appear in weekly plans.
4. **Check in often.** A short weekly check beats a long quarterly post-mortem.
5. **Score candidly.** Missing a stretch goal is information, not failure.

## Structure
| Part | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Objective | Qualitative, motivating direction | Become the go-to SEO lead for fintech |
| Key result | Measurable outcome with a baseline and target | Publish 6 case studies (from 0) |
| Key result | | Grow newsletter subscribers from 800 to 2,000 |
| Key result | | Speak at 2 industry events |
| Initiatives | Projects that drive key results | Case study series; event pitch list |
| Habits | Recurring actions | Write 3 hours every Tuesday |
| If-then plans | Triggers for action | If it is Tuesday 9am, then I write for 3 hours |

Three to five key results per objective; each with a baseline, a target and a date.

## Goal types
| Type | Use | Scoring |
|---|---|---|
| Committed | Must happen (deadlines, obligations) | Expect 100% |
| Stretch (aspirational) | Ambitious, uncertain | Reaching about 60 to 70% is often a good result |
| Learning | New skill or exploration | Milestones completed |
| Habit | Behaviour to build | Consistency over weeks |

## When to use, and when not to
Use it at the start of a year, quarter or new role, when goals feel vague or too many, for team goal setting, career planning and when weekly work feels disconnected from bigger aims.

Don't use it for company-wide OKR programmes across many teams (the Product Management category) or for daily task choices (`prioritisation`).

## Inputs
- Time period (quarter or year) and life or work areas in scope.
- What matters most and why; any longer-term vision.
- Current baselines (numbers, skills, situations).
- Constraints: time, money, energy, commitments.
- Last period's goals and results, if any.

## Workflow

### Step 1. Look back
What was achieved last period, what was not and why. Carry forward only what still matters.

### Step 2. Choose areas and objectives
Pick one to three objectives that would make the period a success. Write each as a clear direction a person would be proud to reach.

### Step 3. Write key results
For each objective, three to five measurable outcomes with baseline, target and date. Test each: could someone outside judge whether it happened?

### Step 4. Choose initiatives and habits
The projects and recurring actions most likely to move each key result. Remove anything that does not.

### Step 5. Add if-then plans
For each habit or initiative, a trigger: when, where and what. Include obstacle plans: "If I miss Tuesday, then I write on Thursday morning."

### Step 6. Check capacity
Compare the hours needed with hours available each week. Cut an objective rather than overload.

### Step 7. Set the rhythm
Weekly: one-minute confidence check per key result (on track, at risk, off track) during the weekly review. Monthly: progress against targets and adjustments. End of period: score and reflect.

### Step 8. Score and reflect
Score each key result from 0 to 1.0, write one lesson per objective and carry useful work into the next period.

## Key result quality test
| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| Improve SEO | Grow organic sign-ups from 1,200 to 1,800 a month |
| Work on fitness | Run 5 km in under 30 minutes (now 34) |
| Learn SQL | Build 3 reporting queries used weekly by the team |
| Network more | Have 12 conversations with people in target companies |

## Output format
```
# Goals: <person or team>, <period>
## Look back (wins, misses, lessons)
## Objectives and key results (baseline, target, date)
## Initiatives and habits per key result
## If-then plans, including obstacle plans
## Capacity check
## Check-in rhythm and scoring method
```

## Writing objectives
A good objective is short, specific enough to guide choices and motivating to read. Compare "Do marketing" with "Make our onboarding the best in our market". It should point to one area; if it needs "and", consider two objectives or choosing one.

## Leading and lagging measures
Lagging measures, such as revenue or a new job offer, show results but arrive late. Leading measures, such as weekly outreach messages or published case studies, move earlier and are within the person's control. Pair each lagging key result with one leading measure tracked weekly, so problems show up in week three rather than week twelve.

## Personal life goals
The same structure works outside work: health, money, relationships and learning. Keep personal objectives private if preferred, and avoid more than one or two at a time alongside work goals. Health or financial goals that involve medical or investment decisions should be checked with a qualified professional.

## Team OKRs in brief
For a small team, set three to five team objectives, give each key result one owner, share them in one document, and run a 15-minute weekly check-in on confidence and blockers. Personal goals can then support the team's key results.

## Goal file template
```
# goals.md (Q<n> <year>)
Objective 1: <direction>
- KR1: <metric> from <baseline> to <target> by <date> | status: on track
- KR2: ...
Initiatives: ...
Habits and if-then plans: ...
Weekly confidence log: <date> KR1 on track, KR2 at risk (reason)
```

## Edge cases and judgement calls
| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| Too many goals | Rank by importance and keep the top three; park the rest |
| No baseline data | Make the first key result "measure the baseline by week 2" |
| Goal depends on others (a promotion) | Set key results on what the person controls, such as evidence and conversations |
| Life event mid-quarter | Re-plan; drop or shrink objectives openly |
| Team and personal goals conflict | Discuss with the manager; agree which wins this quarter |

## Worked example
A growth marketer wants a senior role within a year. The quarter's objective is "Build a visible track record in lifecycle marketing", with key results: publish 3 case studies (from 0), grow LinkedIn followers in target roles from 1,500 to 3,000, and complete 8 conversations with hiring managers. Initiatives are a case study series and an outreach plan; habits are writing every Tuesday morning and two outreach messages every Thursday. Weekly check-ins mark the outreach result at risk in week 4, so the person moves one writing block to outreach. At the end of the quarter the scores are 1.0, 0.7 and 0.6, and the lessons shape the next quarter.

## Common mistakes
- Goals that are activities, not outcomes.
- Too many objectives.
- No baselines.
- No connection to weekly plans.
- Setting goals in January and never checking again.

## Self-check before you answer
- Are there three or fewer objectives for a person?
- Does every key result have a baseline, target and date?
- Does each key result have initiatives or habits with if-then plans?
- Is there a weekly check-in rhythm?
