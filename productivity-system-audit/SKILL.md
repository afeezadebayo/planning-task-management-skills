# Productivity System Audit

Most people do not have a productivity problem; they have a system problem. Tasks live in five places, the calendar shows meetings but not the work, priorities change daily, nothing gets reviewed, and the to-do list grows until it is ignored. New apps and techniques rarely fix this, because the gaps sit between the parts: capture, decisions, planning, focus and review.

This skill audits how a person or small team plans and gets work done, finds the few breakdowns that cost the most time and stress, and recommends a minimal system and the specialist skills to fix each gap. It is the entry point for the Planning and Task Management set.

## Operating principles
1. **Fewer tools, one source of truth.** Every task should live in one trusted place.
2. **Fix the biggest leak first.** Usually capture, prioritisation or review, not the app.
3. **Fit the person's real week.** Plans must survive meetings, interruptions and energy dips.
4. **Small, sustainable habits.** A 20-minute weekly review beats an elaborate system abandoned in a fortnight.
5. **Measure outcomes, not activity.** Finished priorities and calmer weeks matter more than ticked boxes.

## Audit areas
| Area | Questions | Typical finding |
|---|---|---|
| Capture | Where do tasks and ideas land? How many places? | Tasks spread across email, chat, notes and memory |
| Clarify | Are tasks written as next actions? | Vague items such as "marketing plan" sit for weeks |
| Prioritise | How are the day's and week's priorities chosen? | Urgent requests always win over important work |
| Plan | Is work scheduled, or only meetings? | Calendar full of meetings, no time for priorities |
| Focus | How often is deep work interrupted? | Notifications and chat break every hour |
| Review | Is there a weekly review? | No review; lists go stale |
| Goals | Do daily tasks connect to goals? | Busy weeks with little progress on goals |
| Energy | When is the person sharpest? | Hardest work scheduled in low-energy hours |
| Tools | Which apps are used, and are they trusted? | Three task apps, none trusted |
| Team | How are requests and handoffs managed? | Requests arrive by chat with no owner or date |

## When to use, and when not to
Use it when someone feels busy but behind, is starting a new role, keeps missing deadlines, has too many tools or wants to set up an AI-assisted planning system.

Don't use it for clinical concerns such as burnout, anxiety or attention difficulties; suggest speaking to a qualified professional while still helping with practical planning. Don't use it for team project management at scale (the Product Management category).

## Inputs to ask for, in one message
1. Role, typical week and main goals for the next quarter.
2. Where tasks, notes and requests currently live.
3. A week of calendar (or a description of it).
4. The current to-do list, even if messy.
5. What feels most broken.
6. Tools the person must use for work.

## Workflow

### Step 1. Map the flow
Trace how a request becomes a finished task: where it arrives, where it is captured, how it is decided, when it is scheduled, how it is reviewed.

### Step 2. Score each area
Score each audit area 0 to 3 (0 missing, 1 ad hoc, 2 working, 3 strong) with one line of evidence.

### Step 3. Find the biggest leaks
Pick the two or three lowest-scoring areas that cost the most time or stress.

### Step 4. Design the minimal system
One task list, one calendar, one notes place, one inbox for requests and a fixed weekly review slot. Name the tools.

### Step 5. Set the daily and weekly rhythm
For example: five-minute morning plan, two focus blocks, end-of-day shutdown, 30-minute weekly review.

### Step 6. Plan the rollout
Introduce one change a week for three to four weeks rather than everything at once.

### Step 7. Hand off
Point each gap to a specialist skill.

## Scorecard template
```
| Area | Score (0-3) | Evidence | Fix | Skill |
| Capture | 1 | Tasks in 5 places | Single inbox | task-capture-clarify |
| Review | 0 | No weekly review | Friday 30 minutes | weekly-review |
```

## Evidence behind the rhythm
| Practice | Why it helps |
|---|---|
| Writing a concrete plan for unfinished tasks | Research by Masicampo and Baumeister found that making a specific plan reduced intrusive thoughts about unfinished goals [Research, 2011] |
| If-then plans ("If it is 9am Monday, then I draft the report") | A meta-analysis by Gollwitzer and Sheeran found implementation intentions improved goal attainment with a medium-to-large effect [Research, 2006] |
| Adding buffer to estimates | People routinely underestimate how long tasks take, known as the planning fallacy [Research, Kahneman and Tversky; Buehler and colleagues] |
| Weekly review | Keeps lists trusted, so the mind stops tracking open loops |

## Signs the system is working
- Every task the person cares about can be found in one place.
- Each morning starts with two or three clear priorities.
- At least one focus block a week goes to the most important project.
- Waiting-for items are chased on time.
- The weekly review happens most weeks.
- The person reports less stress about forgotten tasks.

## Team version
For a small team, add: one place where requests arrive, an owner and due date for every request, a short weekly priorities meeting, and visible Waiting for items so handoffs do not stall.

## Edge cases and judgement calls
| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| Meeting-heavy role | Protect two focus blocks a week first; audit meetings |
| Many interruptions by design (support, operations) | Plan in short sprints; batch requests |
| Tool mandated by employer | Work within it; add a personal capture inbox if needed |
| Person wants a new app | Fix habits first; switch only if the tool blocks the system |
| Signs of burnout or overwhelm | Reduce commitments before adding systems; suggest professional support |

## Worked example
A marketing manager felt constantly behind. The audit found tasks in email flags, a notebook, chat messages and two apps; no weekly review; and a calendar full of meetings with no time for the quarterly launch. The plan moved all tasks into one list, rewrote vague items as next actions, blocked two mornings a week for launch work, added a Friday 30-minute review and a five-minute morning plan, introduced one change a week, and connected the list to an AI agent that updates it from meeting notes. Within a month, launch work was on schedule and the backlog had halved.

## Common mistakes
- Switching apps instead of fixing habits.
- Trying every technique at once.
- Planning a perfect day that ignores meetings.
- No weekly review, so the system decays.
- Treating every request as urgent.

## Questions to ask before you recommend changes
- Where does work arrive, and where does it go?
- What did you finish last week that mattered?
- When in the day do you do your best work?

## Self-check before you answer
- Is there one trusted place for tasks?
- Are the top two or three leaks fixed first?
- Does the rhythm fit the person's real week?
- Is the rollout gradual?
